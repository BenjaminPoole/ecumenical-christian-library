VIII.5. To call and dissolve all solemn assemblies about the public affairs of the Church.

VIII.5. To call and dissolve all solemn assemblies about the public affairs of the Church.

15949 min read

V. 1The consuls of Rome Polybius affirmeth2 to have had a kind of regal authority, in that they might call together the senate and people whensoever it pleased them. Seeing therefore the affairs of the Church and Christian religion are public affairs, for the ordering whereof more solemn assemblies sometimes are of as great importance and use, as they are for secular affairs; it seemeth no less an act of supreme authority to call the one than the other. Wherefore amongst sundry other3 prerogatives of Simon’s dominion over the Jews, this4 is reckoned as5 not the least, “that no man might gather any great assembly in the land without him.” For so the manner of Jewish regiment had always been, that whether the cause for which men assembled themselves in peaceable, good, and orderly course6, were ecclesiastical or civil, supreme authority should assemble them. David gathered all Israel together unto Jerusalem, when the ark was to be removed; he assembled the sons of Aaron and the Levites7. Solomon did the like at such time as the temple was to be dedicated8: when the Church was to be reformed, Asa in his time did the same: the same upon like occasions done afterwards by Joas, Ezekias, Josias, and others9.

10The ancient imperial law11 forbiddeth such assemblies as the emperor’s authority did not cause to be made. Before emperors became Christian12, the Church had never any synod general13; their greatest meetings14 consisted15 of bishops and others the gravest in each province. As for the civil governor’s authority, it suffered them only as things not regarded or accounted16 of, at such times as it did suffer them. So that what right a Christian king hath as touching assemblies of that kind we are not able to judge, till we come unto later times, when religion had won the hearts of the highest powers. Constantine (as Pighius17 doth grant) was not only the first that ever did call any general council together, but even the first that devised the calling of them for consultation about the business18 of God. After he had once given the example, his successors19 a long time followed the same; insomuch that S. Jerome, to disprove the authority of a synod which was pretended to be general, useth20 this as a forcible argument21, “Dic quis imperator hanc synodum jusserit convocari.” Their answer hereunto is no answer, which say, “that 22emperors did not this without conference had with 23bishops:” for to our purpose it is enough, if the clergy alone did it not otherwise than by the leave or24 appointment of their sovereign lords and kings.

Whereas therefore it is on the contrary side alleged, that Valentinian the elder25 being requested by Catholic bishops to grant that there might be a synod for the ordering of matters called in question by the Arians, answered, that he being one of the laity might not meddle with such affairs26, and thereupon wished27, that the priests and bishops, to whom the care of those things belonged28, should meet and consult thereof29 by themselves wheresoever30 they thought good: we must together31 with the emperor’s speech weigh the occasion and the32 drift thereof. Valentinian and Valens, the one a Catholic, the other an Arian, were emperors together: Valens the governor of the east, Valentinian33 of the west empire. Valentinian therefore taking his journey from the east part into the west34, and passing for35 that intent through Thracia, the bishops there36 which held the soundness of Christian belief, because they knew that Valens was their professed enemy, and therefore it the other were37 once departed out of those quarters, the Catholic cause was like to find small38 favour, moved presently Valentinian about a council to be assembled under the countenance of his authority; who by likelihood considering what inconvenience might thereby grow39, inasmuch as it could not be but a mean40 to incense Valens the more against them, refused himself to be author of, or present at any such assembly; and of this his denial gave them a colourable reason, to wit, that he was although an emperor, yet a secular person, and therefore not able in matters of so great obscurity to sit as a competent judge; but, if they which were bishops and learned men did think good to consult thereof together, they might. Whereupon when they could not obtain that which they most desired, yet that which was41 granted them42 they took, and forthwith had a council. Valentinian went on towards Rome, they remaining in consultation till Valens which accompanied him returned back; so that now there was no remedy, but either to incur a manifest contempt, or else at the hands even43 of Valens himself to seek approbation of that they had done. To him, therefore, they became suitors: his answer was short, “Either Arianism, or else44 exile, which they would;” whereupon their banishment ensued. Let reasonable men therefore now45 be judges, how much this example of Valentinian doth make against the authority, which we say that sovereign rulers may lawfully have as concerning synods and meetings ecclesiastical.

46The clergy, in such wise gathered together, is an ecclesiastical senate, which with us, as in former times the chiefest prelate at his discretion did use to assemble, so47 afterwards in such considerations as have been before specified, it seemed48 more meet to annex the said prerogative unto the crown. The plot of reformed discipline not liking hereof49 so well, taketh order that every former assembly before it break50 up should itself appoint both the time and place of their after meeting again. But because I find not any thing on that side particularly alleged against us herein, a longer disputation about so plain a cause shall not need.
