PFFORTS to publish the Latin works of Zwingli in English ■■— ’ translations have extended over a number of years. In 1908, the Rev. Prof. Samuel Macauley Jackson, D.D., of New York University, offered translations of certain Latin works of Zwingli in Ms. form to the General Synod of the Reformed Church in the United States, convened at York, Pennsylvania, for publication. The offer was referred to a special committee, consisting of the Rev. Drs. Good and Richards and of the Elders Loucks, Siebert and Wood. They were instructed “to make special efforts to carry the proposal into execution.”^ At the next meeting of the General Synod of the Reformed Church, held in 1911, at Canton, Ohio, the committee reported that, owing to their failure to secure a sufficient number of advance subscribers, they felt unable to proceed, but that Dr. Jackson himself would publish these works in a different form, for which undertaking he asked the co-operation of the General Synod. ^

In September, 1912, the first volume of this series left the press. The page proof had been read by Dr. Jackson ; but he died at Washington, Connecticut, on the second of August, 1912, a month before the bound copies were delivered.

In a circular issued by Messrs. G. P. Putnam’s Sons and signed by Dr. Jackson, under the date of March 29, 1912, he had announced : ”The undersigned has made arrangements that in case his own editorial labors should be interfered with by invalidism or death, the work will be brought to completion under the supervision of the American Society of Church History and

At its annual meeting in December, 1912, the American Society of Church History took action recorded in the following extract from its minutes: ”The Society gave its formal approval to the plan of the late Dr. Jackson as laid down in the circular which he issued in March, 1912, concerning the continuance of the publication of the translation of the Latin Works, etc. of Zwingll, under the auspices of this Society and under the editorship of its present Secretary. It was, however, voted that this should not be considered a publication of the Society, as in that case the Constitution would demand its free distribution to all members. It was further voted that the Society should assume no financial responsibility whatever for the undertaking.”^ Had the Society possessed an endowment, it would have been able to act with greater freedom.

Dr. Jackson’s executors, Professor George T. Jackson, M.D., and Frederick W. Jackson, Esq., brother and nephew respectively of the deceased, informed the undersigned that the testator had not made the printing of the Zwingli translations mandatory upon his estate. They called, however, for estimates of its cost, and these seemed to be prohibitively high.

After the death of Dr. George T. Jackson, which occurred in New York City on Monday, January 3, 1916, the undersigned continued negotiations with the surviving executor. Through the kind offices of George C. Sprague, Esq.,- a personal friend of the late Samuel Macauley Jackson, an agreement was drawn up between Frederick W, Jackson, Esq., as surviving executor and the American Society of Church History, Inc. This was ratified at a special meeting of the Council of the Society, held in New York City on the 16t.h of February, 1918 ; by the 17th of April it had received the requisite signature.^

This agreement transferred to the American Society of Church History all the printed copies of the first volume of the set together with the copyright thereof; also the unpublished manuscripts and the working library^ used in connection therewith and the right to reprint any article from Dr. Jackson’s ”Selections from Zwingli,” to which he held the copyright. The Society, on its part, agreed to “publish the remainder of the said Works of Huldreich Zwingli, or such parts thereof as in its discretion it shall consider advisable, at as early a date as is reasonably practicable, in view of the demand therefor.” The Society agreed to continue the publications along the general lines contemplated by the late Dr. S. M. Jackson and to cause his name to appear on the cover and title page of each succeeding volume; and to pay to the estate of Dr. Jackson fifty per cent, of the gross amount of all sales of the first volume of the set. Previous to the signing of the agreement with the surviving executor, the American Society of Church History had entered into negotiations with the Rev. Rufus W. Miller, D.D., Secretary of the Publication and Sunday School Board of the Reformed Church in the United States. In 1917-18 arrangements were completed whereby the publication will be continued by this organization under its trade name of the Heidelberg Press, without involving the American Society of Church History in financial responsibility.

This happy solution of the problem would never have been reached had it not been for the interest taken by the officials of the Heidelberg Press and by the Rev. President George W. Richards, D.D., of the Theological Seminary of the Reformed Church at Lancaster, Pa., and above all, by the Rev. Prof. James I. Good, D.D., of Philadelphia, who as chairman of the special committee of the General Synod of the Reformed Church, had most effectively co-operated with the American Society of Church History to make the publication of the theological works of Zwingli possible. At the meeting of the General Synod of the Reformed Church, held in 1917, at Dayton, Ohio, Dr. Good reported that sufficient money and pledges had

The latter volume is the one now issued and the following denominations had part in making its publication possible : The Presbyterian Church in the United States of America, the Presbyterian Church in the United States (Southern), the United Presbyterian Church, the Presbyterian Church in Canada, the Reformed Church in America and the Reformed Church in the United States. The generous assistance of all these churches is hereby gratefully acknowledged.

These appropriations are simply to help pay the cost of publication ; the very modest honorarium of the editors will be met by funds privately raised by Dr. Good. If it should seem desirable to prepare new translations of the German Works, and to revise the inadequate manuscript version of the Correspondence, additional money will be required.

The scope of the translation was stated by Dr. Jackson in his circular of March 29, 1921, as follows: ‘To bring out a series comprising, in not less than six volumes of about 100,000 words each, printed in royal octavo, all the Latin treatises and also the more important of the German treatises of Zwingli, and in addition the complete Zwingli correspondence.”

Careful computations made by the undersigned after Dr. Jackson’s death, at the request of his executors, showed that the series would be considerably longer and much more expensive than Dr. Jackson had estimated. It was decided, therefore, to revert to Dr. Jackson’s original plan of presenting the Latin treatises of Zwingli, and to postpone the revision and printing of the Correspondence, and the translation of additional German tracts, until funds should be available for those purposes.

Jackson desired that it should represent the latest critical text and held back those tracts already translated on the basis of Schuler and Schulthess, for ten years, in order to benefit by the labors of Egli and Finsler.^

Of the Tracts included in the present volume none have as yet appeared in the critical edition ; all are based on Schuler and Schulthess. As early as 1916 the present writer, foreseeing this eventuality, consulted Professor Finsler, who wrote a letter from Basel on the 27th of February, 1916, saying (in German) : ”In my opinion you may use for the purpose of your translation in those cases where Tracts have not yet appeared in our edition of Zwingli, either the original prints or the edition of Schuler and Schulthess. About the individual prints my Zwingli-Bibliographie (Zurich, 1897), gives you exact information.”

Professor Finsler went on to say that the “Account of the Faith of Zwingli” would be presented in volume V, number 163 ; that the “Exposition of the Christian Faith” would be in volume VI, number 181 ; and that the ”Letter to the Illustrious Princes of Germany Assembled at Augsburg” would be in volume VI, number 167 ; and these would be ready in about five, seven and six years respectively. He continued: “For these three items there are good original prints; and these are also reprinted in such a way in the two complete editions and in Schuler and Schulthess that you may, in my opinion, calmly base your translation on the said editions. I regret that our own edition cannot be of service to you yet.”

As all but one of these Tracts were printed in Zwingli’s lifetime and thus received Zwingli’s own imprimatur, their printed text is superior to any possible divergent manuscript

It is greatly to be hoped that publication may be resumed, in which case American subscriptions would be most appropriate.

In continuing the publication of the Latin Tracts we have rehictantly decided to abandon the chronological order ; instead we present Zwingli’s Latin Works in topical groups. As will be seen from its Table of Contents, the present volume (II) contains in the sequence of their dates certain comprehensive Statements of Zwingli’s beliefs, such as An Account of the Faith of Huldrelch Zwingli presented to Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Augsburg. The inclusion of John Eck’s refutation thereof presents material indispensable in judging Zwingli’s divergence from Roman Catholicism as seen by contemporaries. To prevent this attack on Zwingli from being carelessly mistaken for one of his own productions it is printed in smaller type.

The translations in the present volume were made (with one exception) by Mr. Henry Preble, formerly an assistant professor of Latin in Harvard University. They have been carefully revised and annotated by Professor William J. Hinke, Ph.D., D.D., of the Auburn Theological Seminary, a wellknown writer on the history of the Reformed Church in the United States.

The third volume will contain Zwingli’s longest systematic treatise, Of True and False Religion (1525). This deserves a place in the history of Protestant theology between Melanchthon’s Loci of 1521 and Calvin’s Institutio of 1536. In this volume there will also be presented in translation: Adversus Hieronymwm Ejuserum, canonis missae adsertorem, Huldrychi Zuinglii antibolon} Though belonging by topic to the Writings on the Eucharist, which Professor Robert Hastings Nichols, Ph.D., of the Auburn Theological Seminary will edit as the fourth volume of this series, the Antiholon is quoted so frequently and at such length in Of True and False Religion that it seems best to us to transfer it to the third volume, thus incidentally relieving the great pressure on the space of the fourth.
