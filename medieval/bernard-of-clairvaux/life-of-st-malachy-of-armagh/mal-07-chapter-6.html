Chapter 6

Chapter 6

c. 114934 min read

St. Malachy’s Apostolic Labours, Praises and Miracles.

  1. (23). Malachy embarked in a ship, and after a prosperous voyage landed at his monastery of Bangor,1 so that his first sons might receive the first benefit.2 In what state of mind do you suppose they were when they received their father—and such a father—in good health from so long a journey? No wonder if their whole heart gave itself over to joy at his return, when swift rumour soon brought incredible gladness even to the tribes3 outside round about them. In fine, from the cities and castles and hamlets they ran to meet him, and wherever he turned he was received with the joy of the whole land.4 But honour is not to his taste. He exercises his office as legate; many assemblies are held in many places, so that no region, or part of a region, may be defrauded of the fruit and advantage of his legation. He sows beside all waters;5 there is not one who can escape from his sedulous care. Neither sex, nor age, nor condition, nor [religious] profession is held in account.6 Everywhere the saving seed is scattered, everywhere the heavenly trumpet sounds. He scours every place, everywhere he breaks in, with the sword of his tongue unsheathed to execute vengeance upon the nations and punishments upon the peoples.7 The terror of him is on them that do evil.8 He cries unto the unrighteous, deal not unrighteously, and to the wicked, lift not up the horn.9 Religion is planted everywhere, is propagated, is tended. His eyes are upon them,10 his care is for their necessities. In councils, which are everywhere held, the ancient traditions are revived, which, though their excellence was undisputed, had fallen into disuse by the negligence of the priests. And not only are the old restored, new customs are also devised; and whatsoever things he promulgated are accepted as though issued from heaven, are held fast, are committed to writing for a memorial to posterity. Why should we not believe those things were sent from heaven which so many heavenly miracles confirm? And that I may make what has been said credible, let me touch on some of these miracles in a few words. For who can enumerate all? Though I confess I had rather dwell on those things which can be imitated than on those which can only excite wonder.

  2. (24). And in my judgement the first and greatest miracle that he exhibited was himself.11 For to say nothing of his inner man,12 the beauty and strength and purity of which his habits and life sufficiently attested, he so bore himself even outwardly in a uniform and consistent manner, and that the most modest and becoming, that absolutely nothing appeared in him which could offend the beholders. And, indeed, he who offends not in word, the same is a perfect man.13 But yet in Malachy, who, though he observed with unusual care, ever detected, I will not say an idle word,14 but an idle nod? Who ever knew his hand or his foot to move without purpose? Yea, what was there that was not edifying in his gait, his mien, his bearing, his countenance? In fine, neither did sadness darken nor laughter turn to levity the joyousness of his countenance.15 Everything in him was under discipline, everything a mark of virtue, a rule16 of perfection. Always he was grave, but not austere. Relaxing at times, but never careless; neglecting nothing, though for a time ignoring many things. Quiet often, but by no means at any time idle.17 From the first day of his conversion to the last of his life, he lived without personal possessions.18 He had neither menservants nor maidservants,19 nor villages nor hamlets, nor in fact any revenues, ecclesiastical or secular, even when he was a bishop. There was nothing whatever ordained or assigned for his episcopal mensa, by which the bishop might live; for he had not even a house of his own. But he was almost always going about all the parishes20 serving the Gospel,21 and living of the Gospel,22 as the Lord appointed for him when he said, The labourer is worthy of his hire.23 Except that more frequently, making the Gospel itself without charge,24 as a result of the labours of himself and his companions, he brought with him that by which he might sustain himself and those who laboured with him in the work of the ministry.25 Further, if at times he had to rest he did so in the holy places which he himself had scattered through the whole of Ireland; but he conformed to the customs and observances of those with whom it pleased him to tarry, content with the common life and the common table. There was nought in his food, nought in his clothing, by which Malachy could be distinguished from the rest of the brethren; to such a degree, though he was greatest, did he humble himself in all things.26

  3. Then, when he went out to preach, he was accompanied by others on foot, and on foot went he himself, the bishop and legate. That was the apostolic rule; and it is the more to be admired in Malachy because it is too rare in others. The true successor of the Apostles assuredly is he who does such things. But it is to be observed how he divides the inheritance with his brothers,27 equally descendants of the Apostles. They lord it among the clergy;28 he, though he was free from all men, made himself the servant of all.29 They either do not preach the Gospel and yet eat, or preach the Gospel in order that they may eat; Malachy, imitating Paul, eats that he may preach the Gospel.30 They suppose that arrogance and gain are godliness;31 Malachy claims for himself by inheritance labour and a load.32 They believe themselves happy if they enlarge their borders;33 Malachy glories in enlarging charity.34 They gather into barns35 and fill the wine-jars, that they may load their tables; Malachy collects [men] into deserts and solitudes that he may fill the heavens. They, though they receive tithes and first-fruits and oblations, besides customs and tributes by the gift of Cæsar and countless other revenues, nevertheless take thought what they shall eat or what they shall drink;36 Malachy having nothing of such things, yet makes many rich37 out of the store-house of faith. Of their desire and anxiety there is no end; Malachy, desiring nothing, knows not how to think about the morrow.38 They exact from the poor that which they may give to the rich; Malachy implores the rich to provide for the poor. They empty the purses of their subjects; he for their sins heaps altars39 with vows and peace-offerings.40 They build lofty palaces, raise up towers and ramparts to the heavens.41 Malachy, not having where to lay his head,42 does the work of an evangelist.43 They ride on horses44 with a crowd of men, who eat bread for nought, and that not their own;45 Malachy, hedged round with a college of holy brothers, goes about on foot, bearing the bread of angels,46 with which to satisfy the hungry souls.47 They do not even know the congregations;48 he instructs them. They honour powerful men and tyrants; he punishes them. O, apostolic man, whom so many and so striking signs of his apostleship49 ennoble! What wonder, then, if he has wrought wondrous things when he himself is so wonderful? Yet truly not he but God in him.50 Moreover, it is said, Thou art the God that doest wonders.51

  4. (25). There was a woman in the city of Coleraine52 who had a demon. Malachy was called; he prayed for the possessed; he commanded the invader and he went out. But his iniquity was not yet fully satisfied, and he entered into an unhappy woman who happened to be standing by. And Malachy said, “I did not release that woman from your grasp in order that you might enter this one; go out of her also.” He obeyed, but went back to the former woman; and driven forth from her once more, he again went into the second. So for some time he vexed them alternately, fleeing to and fro. Then the saint, indignant that he was mocked by a demon, summoned up his spirit, and shouted; and when he had made an attack on the adversary with all the forces of faith, he drove the demon away from both, no less vexed than those whom he had vexed. But do not suppose, reader, that the delay which he caused the saint was due to his own strength: it was permitted by the divine dispensation, evidently in order that by this as well the power of the evil one as the victory of Malachy might be made more manifest.
